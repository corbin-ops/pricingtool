Why Micro Deals Demand Lower Offers — And It Is Not Optional
When you are working a micro deal — a parcel with a market value under $30,000 — you are operating in a
mathematically unforgiving environment. The numbers are not just tight; they are actively working against you
unless you structure the offer correctly. Here is what is happening under the hood, and why the 40% floor on micro
deals is not pessimism — it is just arithmetic.
The Two-Cost Problem: One Scales, One Does Not


--- PAGE 2 ---
Every land deal carries two categories of transaction cost: realtor commission and closing costs. The critical thing to
understand is that these two costs behave completely differently as deal size changes — and that difference is the
entire reason the offer range expands as you move up the tiers.
Realtor commission is percentage-based. On a $20,000 sale at 8%, that is $1,600. On a $500,000 sale at 4.5%,
that is $22,500. The dollar amount grows with the deal, but the commission always stays proportional — it never
punishes small deals disproportionately. That part is fair.
Closing costs are a flat fee. Whether you close on a $20,000 parcel or a $500,000 farm, the title company, the
attorney, the recording fees, and the administrative costs still add up to roughly $3,000 to $4,500. That number
barely moves regardless of deal size. On a $20,000 deal, that flat $3,500 represents 17.5% of your entire sale price
— before you have even touched the realtor commission. Add 8% commission ($1,600) and you have already
given up $5,100, or about 25% of the sale, before a single dollar reaches your pocket.
What This Means for Your Offer
On that same $20,000 deal, if you offered 70% of market value — which sounds reasonable and even conservative
by standard rubric thinking — you would have paid $14,000 to acquire the property. After costs of $5,100, you walk
away with roughly $900. That is not a business. That is a lot of work and risk for less than a thousand dollars. To
make a meaningful net profit on a micro deal — say $4,000 to $6,000 — you need to buy at 50% to 60% of market
value, sometimes lower. The 40% floor on the tier card exists for exactly this reason: it is the minimum cushion
required for costs to not destroy the deal.
Now compare that to a $400,000 deal. Closing costs are roughly $5,500 — which is only 1.4% of the sale price.
Realtor commission at 4.5% is $18,000. Total costs: about $23,500, or under 6% of the deal. Even if you offered
88% of market value, you are still walking away with over $20,000 net. The math works because the fixed cost
problem disappears at scale. Closing costs that would have consumed 17% of a micro deal are now a rounding
error.
The Unknown Costs Factor
There is a third variable that makes micro deals even more demanding: unexpected costs. A survey discrepancy, a
title issue, a small lien, a boundary encroachment — these things happen, and they cost money to resolve. On a
large deal where your margin is $40,000 or more, a $2,000 surprise is manageable. On a micro deal where your
entire net is $4,500, that same $2,000 surprise wipes out nearly half your profit. This is why micro deals require
discipline. You are not being cheap with the seller — you are building in the buffer that the math demands. Every
percentage point you give away on a small deal costs you far more, proportionally, than it would on a large one.

06
KNOW YOUR POSITION: TIME AS A TOOL
The First Question Is Not the Price — It Is the Competition


--- PAGE 7 ---
Before you decide how to structure an offer, before you even think about the number, you need to answer
one question: is anyone else in this deal? That single piece of information changes everything — not just the
price, but the entire strategy, the timing, and how much risk you are taking on. Skipping this step is one of the
most expensive mistakes a field agent can make.
When Time Is Your Friend — No Competition Detected
If you have done your homework and there is no other buyer in the picture, time stops being a threat and
becomes your single most powerful tool. Here is what actually happens when there is no competition: you
make your offer — a real, specific, meaningful number like $20,000 or $80,000 or $150,000 — and then you
go quiet. You do not follow up the next day. You do not lower the number. You do not pressure. You let the
number sit.
And here is the psychology of why this works. That number — whatever it is — is very likely the largest
single dollar amount anyone has ever offered this person for that piece of land. It starts replaying in their
head. They tell their spouse. They think about what they could do with it. They start doing the math on what
their life looks like with that money in it. Every day that passes without another offer is a day that your
number gets bigger in their mind, not smaller. You are not being ignored — you are letting compounding
psychology do the work for you. This is why patience in a no-competition environment is not just acceptable
— it is the correct play.
When Time Is Not Your Friend — Competition Changes Everything
Now flip the scenario. Another buyer is in the picture — or you have reason to believe one might be. This
changes the entire equation immediately. The problem with competition is not just that someone else might
win the deal. The problem is that you cannot see what they are offering. You do not know if they are at $500
less than you, or $5,000 less, or $5,000 more. You do not know if they are a cash buyer who can close in two
weeks, or if they are stringing the seller along. You cannot gauge their timeline, their intent, or their number.
And that means any strategy built on waiting becomes a coin flip — and coin flips are not a business model.
When competition is real, the decision framework narrows fast. You either have conviction in your number
and you move — cash close, clean terms, fast timeline — or you assess that the deal does not make sense
at a competitive price and you walk. What you do not do is wait and hope. Hoping is not a strategy. A deal
lost to a competitor who offered $500 more is still a lost deal, and you will never know it was that close. Move
with information or move on.
How to Gauge Competition — Ask Without Asking
The most important skill here is not negotiation — it is information gathering. You need to know if there is
competition, and you need to find out in a way that does not tip your hand or make the seller feel
interrogated. The approach is casual, almost indifferent. Something like: "I know you're looking to sell this,
but honestly, you're not really moving on any of the offers you're getting, are you?" — said lightly, like you
already know the answer and are just making conversation.


--- PAGE 8 ---
That kind of question does two things at once. It implies you are relaxed and not desperate — which is
exactly the posture you want. And it opens a door for the seller to either confirm that no one is really biting
(your green light to be patient) or to reveal that someone else is actually close (your signal to decide fast).
The specific words and the conversation around this will be covered in detail by Yousef — but the underlying
principle is this: gathering information before committing to a strategy is not optional. It is the job. Know your
position first. Then decide how to play it.

THE CONTINGENCY FRAMEWORK — NEVER PAY OUT OF POCKET
The Non-Negotiable Foundation of Every Deal
There is one principle that sits underneath every deal structure in this business, and it is not optional. You do
not pay for anything out of pocket unless a closing is already guaranteed and money is already coming in on
the other end. Not the property. Not the perk test. Not the survey. Not a single dollar leaves your account
speculatively. Every cost gets structured so that it either falls on the seller, gets reimbursed at the closing
table, or simply does not happen if the deal does not close. This is not negotiation style — it is how the
business is built.
The Property Itself Is Contingent


--- PAGE 9 ---
The clearest example of this principle is the deal itself. On a wholesale or double close, you do not purchase
the property and then hope to find a buyer. That sequence — buy first, sell second — is backwards and
exposes you to real financial risk. The correct sequence is: get the property under contract, find a buyer who
commits, then close. In a double close, you buy and sell on the same day, often using the buyer's funds to
fund your side of the purchase. You have never carried the property. You have never been exposed. The
whole deal is contingent on having someone on the other end before you commit to the first end.
If you cannot find a buyer, you do not close. The property stays with the seller — it is still their land, and they
have lost nothing except time. This is important to understand as a framing tool as well: when sellers push
back on your terms, you can honestly tell them that if this does not work out, their land is exactly where it
was, still theirs, still on the market. You are not taking anything from them by walking away.
Perk Tests — Structured So the Risk Stays With the Land
A perk test — short for percolation test — determines whether a piece of land can support a septic system,
which is often a prerequisite for development or resale value. The test costs money, and the outcome is not
guaranteed. This creates a simple problem: if you pay for the test and it fails, you have spent money on a
deal that no longer makes sense, and the seller still owns the land. That is a loss with no upside.
The solution is to negotiate the perk test as a contingency cost. There are two clean approaches. Option A:
the seller pays for the test upfront, since it is still their property and the results benefit them regardless of
whether the deal closes — they learn something real about their land. Option B: you pay for it, but with a
written agreement that the cost is reimbursed to you at the closing table. If the deal does not close, you
absorb it — which is why Option A is preferred. Either way, you are framing this correctly to the seller: "This
test tells you more about your own property. If it passes, great — we close and you get reimbursed or I cover
it. If it fails, at least you know, and it cost you a test instead of a bad deal." That framing is honest and it
usually lands.
Surveys — Pay at the Closing Table, Not Before
Surveys follow the same logic. A survey is often required to confirm boundaries, identify encroachments, or
satisfy a buyer's due diligence. It has real value, but that value only materializes if the deal closes. The goal
is to find surveyors who understand your business model and are willing to get paid at the closing table
rather than upfront. These relationships exist, and building them is part of running this operation correctly. A
surveyor who works with investors and understands the contingency model is a long-term asset.
If the deal falls through, have an honest conversation with the surveyor. Most professionals who work in this
space understand that closings are not guaranteed. The key is to set that expectation at the start of the
relationship, not after the fact. And again — the framing for the seller is the same: this survey benefits them.
If the deal does not close, they now have a current survey on their property. That is not nothing.
The Verbal Agreement Comes First — Then It Goes in Writing


--- PAGE 10 ---
Every one of these contingencies starts as a conversation. You negotiate verbally — casually,
matter-of-factly, like this is just how things are done, because it is. Once the seller agrees, it goes into the
agreement in writing. Your standard contract has a format; these contingency terms get added on top of that.
The written agreement protects everyone and removes ambiguity at the closing table. Nothing about this
framework is adversarial — it is just clean business. You are not asking the seller to take on your risk. You
are asking them to acknowledge the shared reality that nothing is guaranteed until it closes, and structuring
the deal so that reality is reflected in who pays for what and when.

STAGE 1 — HOT
Seller is asking at or below 50% of market value. This is the deal the entire system is built to find. Move immediately
— do not wait, do not overthink it. Your margin is clean, your costs are covered, and there is real money on the
table. The relationship here is straightforward: be professional, be responsive, and be easy to work with. Sellers in
this bucket are motivated and ready. Your job is not to negotiate — it is to execute cleanly and not give them a
reason to second-guess you. Speed and reliability close Hot deals. Hesitation loses them.
STAGE 2 — WARM
Seller is asking above 50% of MV but at or below your maximum offer dollar amount. The math works but the margin
is thinner than you want. Your goal here is to negotiate down toward 50% while knowing you have a real ceiling to
protect you. Relationship matters here — the more trust and rapport you have built, the more likely they are to move
on price. People do not discount for strangers. If they like you, trust you, and believe you will actually close, they will
give you more room than they would give a faceless offer. Push toward your target, stay warm, and never make
them feel pressured. Pressure kills Warm deals.
STAGE 3 — REJECTED — CLOSE


--- PAGE 11 ---
Seller is asking between your maximum offer dollar amount and 10% above it. You cannot make money at their
number today, but the gap is small and closable. This is your most valuable follow-up bucket — treat it that way.
Touch them once a month, but not to reopen a hard negotiation. Keep it casual and relational. Check in, ask how
things are going, stay present in their life. Your number is already in their head and every month that passes without
another serious buyer makes it sound better. The relationship is the strategy here — you are not pushing, you are
staying warm while time does the work. If after three monthly touches they have not moved at all, evaluate dropping
them to Far A.
STAGE 4 — REJECTED — FAR A
Seller is asking between 10% and 15% above your maximum offer. The gap is real but not permanent — one shift in
their circumstances and they could be back in Warm territory overnight. A failed retail listing, a tax bill, a family
situation, financial pressure — any one of these can move a Far A seller fast when it happens. Follow up every three
months and keep the tone light and relational. You are not calling to renegotiate, you are calling to stay in their life.
Ask how the property is going, whether they have had any interest, how things are on their end. Plant the seed
without pressure. When the moment comes, you want to be the first call they make.
STAGE 5 — REJECTED — FAR B
Seller is asking between 15% and 20% above your maximum offer. The gap here cannot close on a single life event
alone — it needs multiple things to go wrong before their expectation becomes realistic. You are not actively
pursuing this deal, you are maintaining a long-term presence at minimal cost. Follow up every six months. Keep it
brief and genuinely human — not a sales call, just a check-in. The relationship you are building here is a slow burn.
Some of these sellers will never move. A few of them will eventually hit a wall and remember that you were the one
who kept showing up without pressure. That is what you are investing in. Do not spend real energy here — just stay
visible.
STAGE 6 — ARCHIVE — AT MARKET
Seller is asking between 95% and 105% of market value. They want what any retail buyer would pay them and there
is no room for your business model at that price. This is not a negotiation problem — it is a psychology problem.
They do not yet believe they need to discount. The most likely path to a deal here is a failed retail listing — they try
the MLS, it sits, buyers push back on price, and eventually reality sets in. Follow up every six months. Keep the tone
warm and completely pressure-free. You are not trying to change their mind today. You are staying in their
peripheral vision so that when retail fails — and for motivated sellers it often does — your name is still there.
STAGE 7 — ARCHIVE — OVER MARKET
Seller is asking between 105% and 115% of market value. Their expectation is detached from reality and closing
that gap takes time and usually a painful process of failed retail attempts. There is nothing you can say in a
conversation today that will move them — they need the market to tell them what you already know. Follow up once
a year. Keep it short and friendly. The relationship here is one of pure patience — you are not a priority to them and
they should not be one to you. Never argue about price or try to convince them they are wrong. That kills the
relationship permanently. Stay cordial, stay light, and let time do the work.


--- PAGE 12 ---
STAGE 8 — DEAD LEAD
Seller is asking above 115% of market value. There is no realistic path to a deal here under any normal
circumstances. The gap between their expectation and market reality — let alone your offer — is too large for any
reasonable timeframe. Do not follow up on a schedule. Remove them from your active mental bandwidth entirely. If
they reach out to you, take the call — life circumstances occasionally make the impossible possible — but you
initiate nothing. Your follow-up time and energy are finite. Every minute spent here is a minute taken from a Hot,
Warm, or Rejected Close lead that actually has a path to closing.
